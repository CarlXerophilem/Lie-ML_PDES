\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This dissertation starts with an independent replication of a published
printed scalar Saint-Venant--Exner (SVE) symmetry calculation. Its displayed
source equations (24)--(33) are checked against the original scalar PDE;
corrected local scaling and traveling profile equations, exact first
integrals and constructed numerical IVPs are distinguished from refuted
source formulas. The printed scalar, advectively corrected scalar and full
bed-field systems remain separate. Reconstructed particle position,
velocity and normalized depth are local calculated fields, not measured
flow data or a global solution theorem.

The next stage derives a Lie-point reducer from arbitrary coefficient
functions $\tau(t,s,\phi)$, $\xi(t,s,\phi)$ and $\eta(t,s,\phi)$ through the
prolonged original equation. Determining equations, exceptional parameter
strata, graph rank, chart guards and posed data are checked before a PDE
quotient is admitted. This is an application and implementation of standard
infinitesimal theory, not a claim to have invented it. Reducing independent
variables is distinct from lowering the order of the resulting ODE.

The proposed machine-learning stage seeks a nonconstant first integral or
admitted symmetry of a verified reduced ODE and accepts it only after exact
determining and independence tests on a regular chart. No learned
order-reducing object has yet passed these gates. The previous neural and
spectral profile-fitting campaign remains as bounded historical evidence,
including its failed runs and classical controls; profile approximation alone
does not lower ODE order. The three present research interests are a
Lagrange-coordinate option, exceptional parameter and orbit-rank strata of
symmetry breaking, and task-dependent filtration of unhelpful but possibly
valid symmetries. Hybrid architecture design and conservation analogies are
deferred. None of the finite checks proves an arbitrary-data, continuous
error, shock or full-system result.

\chapter{Replication, discoveries and research design}\label{ch:synopsis}
\section{Problem and organizing principle}
The first question is what survives direct replication of the printed scalar
SVE source \cite{ap}. A source equation number identifies an audit target,
not a separate initial-value problem. The corrected profiles and exact first
integrals are completed work on stated regular charts; singular, undefined
and identity branches remain explicit. The active reproduction package is
\code{research_artifacts/SVE-reproduced-20261012}. The frozen
\code{sve-progress-20261010} campaign and its negative records remain
available as earlier, separate evidence.

The second question is how to derive, rather than import, useful point
generators for a target PDE. The third is whether a learned candidate can
\emph{certifiably lower the order} of an already reduced ODE. This last
question is proposed, not answered by the older profile-fitting runs.
Foundations follow Olver and Oliveri \cite{olver,olver1986,oliveri2026}.
The supplied Olver PDF is the 1986 first edition, with its own page locators;
the main manuscript's 1993 entry denotes a different edition.

\section{Replication result and corrected-solution scope}
\label{sec:dissertation-replication}
The source's formulas (24)--(33) and nine symmetry cases are audited on the
positive chart $\phi_t,\phi_s>0$. The scalar equation admits a corrected
common-scaling reduction for general declared $m$, a cubic-only exceptional
scaling at $m=3$, and regular traveling reductions. The exponent $m=1$ is
also a distinct determining stratum; symmetry count is not monotone in
exponent size. In source case 3.8 the correct invariant graph has a
quadratic \emph{time} profile, not the source's asserted spatial profile.
Some source formula failures coexist with valid special solutions, and the
cubic power law lies on a singular normal-form branch. These distinctions,
the corrected ODEs and source witnesses are documented in the active package.

The constructed corrected-profile IVPs and their sampled $(t,s)$
reconstructions are local numerical illustrations. Positive velocity,
gradient and leading-factor guards apply only where checked. A finite
convergence ladder does not prove continuous accuracy or an arbitrary-data
solution. The exact first integrals in Chapter~\ref{ch:sve} lower ODE order
independently of the reduction from two independent variables to one.

The corrected solution data can be stated directly. For common scaling with
$m=3$, $g=1$, $\kappa=1/30$ and initial jet $(F,F',F'')=(2,1/2,1/10)$
at $z=1$, the local analytic profile begins
\[
 F(1+\xi)=2+\frac{\xi}{2}+\frac{\xi^2}{20}
                -\frac{11\xi^3}{2370}+O(\xi^4),\qquad
 \phi(t,s)=tF(s/t).
\]
The corrected case 3.8 has the exact constructed solution
$\phi(t,s)=s+t+t^2/20$. Equation~\eqref{eq:sve-series-recurrence} gives the
coefficient recurrence, with explicit regularity and residual checks. The
nonlinear Taylor polynomial is a local truncation, not a global closed form.

\section{First-principles reducer and the proposed next stage}
For the scalar PDE $E_m(t,s,\phi^{(n)})=0$, begin with unrestricted smooth
coefficient functions in
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dissertation-master}
 X=\tau(t,s,\phi)\partial_t+\xi(t,s,\phi)\partial_s
   +\eta(t,s,\phi)\partial_\phi,\qquad
 Q=\eta-\tau\phi_t-\xi\phi_s.
\end{equation}
Generate $\pr^{(n)}X$ by total derivatives and impose
$\pr^{(n)}X(E_m)|_{E_m=0}=0$ on each regular branch. Splitting in free jets
produces the determining system; exact substitution checks each candidate.
Only then do $Q=0$, orbit ranks, an inverse quotient chart, the original
PDE pullback and prescribed data decide whether it is a useful reducer.
This is classical Lie theory \cite{olver1986,oliveri2026}, implemented here
with explicit evidence boundaries. The active package's
\code{build_symmetry_report} solves the unrestricted determining problem for
the \emph{printed scalar SVE equation}; \code{derive_reduction} independently
checks selected generators and their profile pullbacks. The source-formula
comparison is a separate reproduction test. There is no implemented
unrestricted determining solver for arbitrary PDEs in this claim.

For a regular reduced ODE $F'=P$, $P'=C$, $C'=R(z,F,P,C)$, a proposed learner
could output $I(z,F,P,C)$. Its acceptance gate is the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dissertation-learned-I}
 I_z+P I_F+C I_P+R I_C=0
\end{equation}
on an open chart, nonconstancy and independence from known integrals, and
$I_C\ne0$ if $I=I_0$ is to solve locally for $C$ and reduce this ODE's order.
Alternatively, a proposed reduced-ODE generator must pass its prolonged
determining equation and produce an actual regular order-reducing quotient.
Neither a small sampled residual nor an accurate fitted profile passes this
gate. No such learned candidate is reported as completed work.

\section{Three research interests and their safeguards}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Lagrange-coordinate option.} The material coordinate $s$ follows
a particle and can make a particular scalar reduction transparent, but a
coordinate choice does not prove equivalence to a full Eulerian bed system.
It is also not a variational Lagrangian. A proposed action must separately
recover the intended equations under Euler--Lagrange variation before
Noether statements are made.
\item \emph{Parameter and distribution of symmetry breaking.} Exceptional
values such as $m=1$ and $m=3$ arise from exact determining factors or
minors. For a chosen generator, the projected orbit rank may change across
the regular coordinate domain; its rank strata determine where a graph
quotient is available. ``Distribution'' here means these strata and their
locations, not a probability law unless a measure is explicitly supplied.
\item \emph{Trivial-symmetry filtration.} A task-dependent sequence checks
equation validity, subgroup closure, projected and total orbit ranks,
invariant graphs, chart denominators, data compatibility and actual solver
value. It is not automatically a filtration by Lie subalgebras or an ideal
quotient. Translation generators can be useful. In the SVE cases, an
undefined divided PDE, a valid vertical generator with no fixed graph,
an identity-only reduction and a valid but data-incompatible quotient have
different statuses; ``trivial'' must not collapse them into ``invalid''.
\end{enumerate}
Hybrid architecture design and conservation analogies are later questions.
The existing exact traveling and scaling first integrals are mathematical
evidence in the replication, not a proposed conservation-analogy result.

\section{The nine analysis stages}
The complete workflow consists of the following stages, each with an explicit
output that the next stage can inspect.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Declare the PDE, parameters, field domains and denominator guards.
\item Construct total derivatives, prolongations and determining equations.
\item Separate parameter rank strata and verify selected generators directly.
\item Filter verified subgroups by effective base rank, regular graph and data
compatibility; certify an adapted chart and inverse for each admitted route.
\item Restrict to invariant jets and derive the reduced differential equation.
\item Test boundary geometry, prescribed data and transformed current/flux.
\item Determine actual ODE order and independently test a first integral;
future learned candidates must pass Equation~\eqref{eq:dissertation-learned-I}
or an equivalent exact reduced-ODE symmetry gate.
\item Compare classical and, where justified, learned profiles with matched
measures; historical profile fitting is not order reduction.
\item Select a solver using admissibility, recorded complexity and workload costs.
\end{enumerate}
The order is logical rather than an excuse to skip a failed gate. For example,
a first integral can be derived before a particular data set is proposed, but
its use in a data-driven solver still requires the data gate. The maintained
HTML workflow links these stages to exact checks and retained experiments.

\section{Questions addressed by the implementation}
The maintained implementation asks whether the resonant parameter behavior can be
recovered without rank decisions based on floating point arithmetic; whether
the common scaling survives a corrected bed transport law; whether the
quotient residual uses the original measure. The earlier learning campaign
asked whether fitted profiles beat spectral controls after setup and
validation costs. That historical experiment does not test discovery of a
new ODE first integral. It also distinguishes accuracy of an IVP state from
accuracy of the third derivative of its dense scalar interpolant.

These questions motivate three chart labels used everywhere in the outputs:
\code{cubic_X5} for the printed cubic benchmark,
\code{common_printed} for common scaling of the printed equation, and
\code{common_corrected} for common scaling of the corrected scalar equation.
They are not interchangeable experiments.

\chapter{Fundamentals: local solutions, jets and symmetry}\label{ch:fundamentals}
\section{Jet spaces and total derivatives}
For a field $u:X\subset\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^q$, the $n$-jet records values
of $u$ and its partial derivatives through order $n$. A multi-index
$J=(J_1,\ldots,J_p)$ represents a symmetric partial derivative of order
$|J|=\sum_iJ_i$. With $e_i$ the $i$th unit multi-index,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total}
D_i=\partial_{x^i}+\sum_{\alpha,J}u^\alpha_{J+e_i}\partial_{u^\alpha_J}.
\end{equation}
Applying $D_i$ to a function of order $n$ generally requires jets of order
$n+1$. An implementation must allocate the needed coordinates or reject the
operation; silently discarding the last term changes the mathematics.
The generic engine checks this finite-order requirement. Symmetric multi-index
storage also permits a direct check that $D_tD_s=D_sD_t$ whenever all required
jets have been allocated.

For a point vector field
\begin{equation}
X=\sum_i\xi^i(x,u)\partial_{x^i}
 +\sum_\alpha\eta^\alpha(x,u)\partial_{u^\alpha},
\end{equation}
its prolongation has coefficients $\eta^\alpha_{\varnothing}=\eta^\alpha$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prolong}
\eta^\alpha_{J+e_i}=D_i\eta^\alpha_J
 -\sum_j u^\alpha_{J+e_j}D_i\xi^j.
\end{equation}
The recurrence is the contact-preserving infinitesimal chain rule. Oliveri's
Theorem 4.6 gives the several-independent-variable version
\cite[Sec.~4.2.2, pp.~91--92]{oliveri2026}. It supplies a practical independent
reference for the code's recurrence, not a replacement for testing it.

\section{Nondegeneracy and the infinitesimal criterion}
A system $\Delta(x,u^{(n)})=0$ defines an equation set in jet space. Its
maximal-rank condition concerns the differential of $\Delta$. Local solvability
concerns whether its allowed jets actually arise from local solution germs.
These are different conditions.

\begin{definition}[Local solvability at a jet]
An equation is locally solvable at an allowed $n$-jet if a smooth solution in
a neighborhood of its base point has exactly that jet. A nondegenerate system
is locally solvable and of maximal rank at every point in the declared
region.
\end{definition}
This is the content used from Olver's Definition 2.70, first edition,
pp.~162--163 \cite{olver1986}. Prescribing a finite jet at a single point
is much weaker than prescribing functions along an initial hypersurface.
It does not assert uniqueness, continuous dependence or a global domain.

For a nondegenerate system and a connected local group, Olver's Theorem 2.71
identifies solution preservation with the infinitesimal condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:criterion}
\pr^{(n)}X[\Delta]\big|_{\Delta=0}=0.
\end{equation}
The local-solvability hypothesis is needed when necessity is interpreted as
a statement about all allowed jets \cite[p.~165]{olver1986}. Solving the
system for leading derivatives facilitates a symbolic on-shell test, but
normal form alone is not a proof of every PDE existence hypothesis.
Oliveri describes eliminating the leading derivatives and splitting the free
jet monomials to obtain linear homogeneous determining equations
\cite[Sec.~4.3, pp.~93--97]{oliveri2026}.

An off-shell multiplier identity, such as $\pr X[\Delta]=a\Delta$, is stronger
as an algebraic statement than a numerical on-shell residual. Both models
below have such common-scaling identities. When denominators are cleared,
their excluded zeros remain guards in the certificate. No symbolic division
by a potentially vanishing parameter is treated as globally valid.

\section{Invariant graphs and transversality}
The characteristic of a point generator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
Q^\alpha=\eta^\alpha-\sum_i\xi^i u^\alpha_i.
\end{equation}
Invariant graphs satisfy $Q=0$. For an order-$n$ equation, the corresponding
jet restriction includes $D_JQ=0$ for $|J|\leq n-1$. Setting only $Q=0$ leaves
higher derivative coordinates unrestricted and can produce an incorrect
reduction.

A graph reduction requires the orbit projection to the independent-variable
space to have the same rank as the orbit in the total space, on a regular
local region. Olver's Proposition 3.37 and Theorem 3.38 discuss this regular,
transverse case \cite[pp.~233--234]{olver1986}. Nontransverse group actions
require additional machinery \cite{anderson2000}; they are not covered by
asserting that every vector field has a useful graph chart.
For common scaling on $t>0$, both the base and total orbit ranks are one.
The origin and the time-zero plane lie outside the logarithmic chart.

\section{Filtering a symmetry for a specific solution task}
The filter is applied per parameter stratum and branch, not to an algebra
dimension alone. First verify the PDE symmetry and subgroup closure; an
inconclusive computation is not a proof of failure. Next require positive
projected orbit rank and equal total and projected orbit ranks for the regular
invariant-graph method used here, then solve all $Q_X=0$ and check the chart.
Finally test the posed domain and data and the equivalence of the pulled-back
equations. A verified reduction enters the cost and accuracy comparison with
the classical or unreduced route; lack of measured benefit is not a failed
symmetry certificate.

The scalar SVE cases distinguish the exits. The vertical translation
$X_3=\partial_\phi$ is a genuine PDE symmetry but has projected rank zero
and $Q=1$, so it has no fixed scalar invariant graph. The source's generic
$X_4$, denoted $X_c$ below, gives a rank-one quotient on $t>0$, but does not
preserve a fixed positive-time boundary. The exceptional $X_5$ fails the
equation-symmetry gate away from $m=3$; this does not remove $X_c$.
This is a task-aware selection path, not a claim that all Lie symmetries have
been classified or that every gate is automated by the current code.

\section{From local solvability to continuous spaces}
A PDE boundary-value theory specifies a domain, a function space, a trace
operator, compatible data, and an existence/stability result. None of these
is supplied by local jet solvability alone. A reduction selects an invariant
subset of potential solutions. To study it in a space such as $C^3([1,1.5])$
or a Sobolev space with meaningful traces, one must also specify the regularity
of the reconstructed fields and the continuity of the chart map.

For the present profiles, $F\in C^3$, $F'>0$, $V>0$ and a nonzero leading
factor define a regular local problem. They become useful uniform assumptions
only if their margins are bounded away from zero on the entire interval and
parameter family. A finite grid checks these conditions at its nodes; it does
not establish their continuous or parameter-uniform validity. The retained
unperturbed tube on $[1,1.4]$ and the new sampled tests on $[1,1.5]$ therefore
carry different evidence labels.

\chapter{Exact determining matrices and parameter strata}\label{ch:strata}
\section{Finite ansatz construction}
Let $X(c)=\sum_{a=1}^N c_aX_a$ be a declared polynomial generator ansatz.
Prolongation is linear in $c$. After substituting the PDE's leading derivative
and splitting independent jet monomials, its determining conditions become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix}
A(\lambda)c=0.
\end{equation}
The implementation verifies linearity before extracting coefficients. It
records the generator basis, remaining parameters, matrix, maximal minors and
specialized nullspaces. A finite polynomial ansatz may omit valid generators
with higher-degree or nonpolynomial coefficients. Closure of a found span is
an additional useful test; closure does not establish maximality either.

\begin{proposition}[Determinantal rank strata in a fixed finite ansatz]
Suppose $A(\lambda)$ has polynomial entries and generic rank $r$ in a
one-dimensional parameter. Then its rank is below $r$ exactly where all
$r\times r$ minors vanish. At each specialization the finite nullity equals
$N-\rank A(\lambda)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The rank of a matrix over a field is the largest order of a nonzero minor.
No minor of order greater than the generic rank is a nonzero polynomial.
Specialization cannot increase that rank. The $r$-minors decide whether the
specialized rank remains $r$. Rank-nullity gives the final statement.
\end{proof}
The univariate engine computes all required maximal minors and their
polynomial gcd. A vanishing gcd identifies the common root candidates;
substituted rank tests determine the actual nullity. A declared enumeration
limit prevents an unbounded combinatorial calculation and reports exhaustion
rather than a mathematical obstruction. Multivariate parameter strata need
ideal methods beyond this univariate API.

\section{Nonlinear diffusion}
Consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diffusion}
u_t=(u^\alpha u_x)_x=u^\alpha u_{xx}+\alpha u^{\alpha-1}u_x^2,
\qquad u>0.
\end{equation}
The implemented nine-coefficient ansatz is
\begin{align}
\tau&=c_0+c_1t+c_2t^2,\qquad
\xi=c_3+c_4x+c_5x^2,\\
\eta&=u(c_6+c_7x+c_8t).
\end{align}
Positive $u$ permits splitting free spatial jets and cancelling known nonzero
powers of $u$. That operation is recorded as a branch assumption. The exact
minor gcd is proportional to $3\alpha+4$. The generic rank is five, hence the
finite nullity is four. At $\alpha=-4/3$ the nullity is five.
One convenient generic span is
\begin{equation}
\partial_t,\quad\partial_x,\quad2t\partial_t+x\partial_x,
\quad\alpha t\partial_t-u\partial_u.
\end{equation}
At resonance it also contains
\begin{equation}
x^2\partial_x-3xu\partial_u.
\end{equation}
Every extracted kernel generator is checked again by prolonging the original
PDE and substituting its equation. This independent check protects against
coefficient-extraction mistakes.

At $\alpha=0$ the PDE is the linear heat equation. This particular ansatz
still has finite nullity four: it intentionally lacks additive solution
translations and several heat generators. The full algebra is infinite for
a different reason, proved in Section~\ref{sec:heat}. Assigning an infinite
nullity to this nine-column matrix would be incorrect.

\section{Quadratic reaction--diffusion}
For
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reaction}
u_t=u_{xx}+\kappa u^2,
\end{equation}
the search takes $\tau$ quadratic in $t$,
$\xi=\tau_t x/2+c_3+c_4t$, and
$\eta=(c_5+c_6x+c_7t)u+c_8+c_9x+c_{10}t$.
It has eleven coefficients. The generic rank is eight and the minor gcd is
proportional to $\kappa^5$. At every tested nonzero coefficient the exact
kernel has dimension three and spans
\begin{equation}
\partial_t,\qquad\partial_x,\qquad
2t\partial_t+x\partial_x-2u\partial_u.
\end{equation}
At $\kappa=0$ the specialized finite nullity is seven, whereas the full heat
algebra is infinite. The distinction again prevents a finite computation
from overclaiming a full classification.

For $\kappa\ne0$, the equivalence change $v=\kappa u$ converts
Equation~\eqref{eq:reaction} to $v_t=v_{xx}+v^2$. It is invertible only away
from $\kappa=0$. It is a transformation between members of an equation class,
not an extra point symmetry at fixed nonzero $\kappa$; field-sign restrictions
must be transformed with it.

\section{Exact infinite heat family}\label{sec:heat}
For the heat operator $\mathcal H[u]=u_t-u_{xx}$,
\begin{equation}
\pr^{(2)}(f(t,x)\partial_u)\mathcal H=f_t-f_{xx}.
\end{equation}
Every heat solution supplies an admitted additive generator. For an explicit
independence witness define
\begin{equation}
f_n(t,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
\frac{n!}{j!(n-2j)!}t^j x^{n-2j},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
\end{equation}
Differentiation and reindexing show $f_{n,t}=f_{n,xx}$.
At $t=0$, $f_n(0,x)=x^n$, so these functions are linearly independent on any
open spatial interval. The code checks the first fifteen members and exports
the general identity and independence argument. Oliveri's heat example and
ReLie output explicitly distinguish the arbitrary heat solution from finite
constant parameters \cite[pp.~96--98, 428]{oliveri2026}.

\chapter{Boundary geometry, data and transformed currents}\label{ch:boundary}
\section{The three compatibility gates}
The reference thesis emphasizes that a symmetry used for a boundary value
problem must preserve the governing equations, the boundary and its data
\cite[Sec.~2.3.4, p.~28]{hijazi}. Let a boundary be defined by
$h(x)=0$ and let $\mathcal B(x,u^{(k)})=0$ denote a prescribed trace condition.
The local infinitesimal checks are
\begin{align}
\pr^{(n)}X\Delta&=0&&\text{on the equation manifold},\\
Xh&=0&&\text{on }h=0,\label{eq:boundarytangent}\\
\pr^{(k)}X\mathcal B&=0&&\text{on }h=0,\ \mathcal B=0.
\end{align}
For a real domain, tangency must be supplemented by preservation of its
inequalities and any other boundary components. For derivative conditions,
relevant differential consequences and equation restrictions may be needed.
These tests provide a local invariance certificate; they do not establish the
existence or uniqueness of the boundary value problem.

A raw action need not vanish off shell. For example, $Xh=h$ proves tangency
because it vanishes on $h=0$. A checker that requires $Xh$ to be the zero
expression would incorrectly reject such a surface. The new boundary module
therefore tests vanishing on the constraint manifold. Polynomial ideal
membership is a sufficient exact certificate; a nonzero ideal remainder is
not in general a complete real-variety nonmembership proof.

All free symbols, including parameters, are polynomial generators in this
test. This prevents the coefficient field from silently dividing by a
parameter that may vanish. Denominators are recorded as guards. A positive
chart factor can be removed from a zero condition, but an unknown parameter
factor is retained. Nonpolynomial restrictions that do not simplify directly
to zero remain unresolved. Inconsistent constraints are rejected when the
polynomial ideal is the unit ideal.

\section{Moving rays and fixed data surfaces}
For common scaling $X_c=t\partial_t+s\partial_s+\phi\partial_\phi$, a moving
ray $h=s-z_0t$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
X_ch=h.
\end{equation}
It is therefore invariant. A trace $\phi-F_0t=0$ on that ray also scales into
itself. In the adapted chart it becomes $F(z_0)=F_0$. The derivative traces
$\phi_s=P_0$ and $t\phi_{ss}=C_0$ become $F'(z_0)=P_0$ and
$F''(z_0)=C_0$. These are constructed data, not generic physical initial data.

A fixed positive-time surface $t=T_0$ has restricted action $T_0\ne0$.
A fixed wall $s=S_0\ne0$ has restricted action $S_0\ne0$. Both fail common
scaling even though the PDE passes it. The plane $t=0$ requires a separate
chart and regularity analysis; it cannot be restored by substituting zero
into $y=\log t$.

The benchmark window $1\leq t\leq2$ is used for quadrature and reconstruction.
Its time caps are not invariant boundary components. Calling the entire
capped rectangle an invariant initial/boundary domain would contradict the
negative control. One may restrict an already constructed solution to that
window, but that is a different operation.

\section{Curvature perturbations and data families}
Changing $C_0$ changes the first-integral constant $I_0=L_m(z_0,F_0,P_0)C_0$.
It consequently changes bed evolution and boundary flux. The family
$C_0=(1+\eta_{\mathrm{ic}})C_{0,\mathrm{base}}$ is accompanied by reconstructed
compatible data for each parameter. It is not a family of solutions to one
unchanged full-data problem. A neural model conditioned on $\eta_{\mathrm{ic}}$
learns this constructed family.

\section{Currents are transformed as vector densities}
Write a scalar conservation equation as $D_tA+D_sJ=0$. For coordinates
$(T,z)=(t,s/t)$, the inverse base Jacobian is $t$, and the transformed current
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:current}
\widetilde A=tA,\qquad\widetilde J=J-zA.
\end{equation}
Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
D_T\widetilde A+D_z\widetilde J=t(D_tA+D_sJ).
\end{equation}
The component $\widetilde J$ is the current paired with the covector $\dd z$
on a ray. No unit-normal or Euclidean metric convention is implied.
For an invariant common-scaling profile, $A=t^{-1}A_\star(z)$ and
$\widetilde J=t^{-1}I_0$. Thus the selected trace/flux data and the integration
constant are linked. The maintained data-flux checker tests this pullback and
several deliberately incompatible controls directly.

\chapter{Scalar SVE models, scaling and order reduction}\label{ch:sve}
\section{Two models with distinct physical interpretations}
Define
\begin{equation}
A[\phi]=\phi_s\phi_{tt}-g\frac{\phi_{ss}}{\phi_s^2},\qquad
T_m[\phi]=\frac{\phi_t^{m-1}\phi_{ts}}{\phi_s}.
\end{equation}
The printed scalar master equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:printed}
\E_m^{\mathrm p}=D_tA-g\beta D_sT_m=0.
\end{equation}
The physical audit starts instead from the corrected coupled equations
\begin{align}
M&=A+g b_s=0,\label{eq:momentum}\\
B&=b_t-\frac{\phi_t}{\phi_s}b_s+\beta T_m=0.\label{eq:bed}
\end{align}
Using $b_s=-A/g$ and equating cross derivatives of $b$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corrected}
\E_m^{\mathrm c}=D_tA-g\beta D_sT_m
 -D_s\left(\frac{\phi_t}{\phi_s}A\right)=0.
\end{equation}
The last term is absent from Equation~\eqref{eq:printed}. The printed equation
is instead the compatibility equation for momentum with nonadvective bed
transport. The supplied shallow-water reference motivates the audit
\cite{ap}; it does not authorize identifying these two equations.

For real $m$, this study uses $\phi_s>0$, $\phi_t>0$ and $g>0$.
The benchmark convention is $\beta=m\kappa$, with $\kappa>0$ held fixed.
Statements using independent nonzero $\beta$ must be distinguished from this
convention, particularly at $m=0$, where the benchmark transport vanishes.

\section{Cubic generator and its off-shell defect}
Let $X_5=t\partial_t-2s\partial_s$. Under its flow, $\phi_t$ has weight $-1$,
$\phi_s$ weight $2$, and $A$ weight zero. Transport divergence has weight
$2-m$. Consequently, for either declared scalar equation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:defect}
\pr^{(3)}X_5\E_m+\E_m=(m-3)g\beta D_sT_m.
\end{equation}
The correction term in Equation~\eqref{eq:corrected} has the same weight as
the time-density term and does not change this defect identity.

On the positive branch with nonzero transport, $D_sT_m$ is not the zero jet
function: the coefficient of $\phi_{tss}$ is
$\phi_t^{m-1}/\phi_s\ne0$. Thus the right-hand side vanishes identically on
the open jet domain if and only if $m=3$. It can vanish on individual jets or
solutions for other $m$; ``identically'' is essential. With $\beta=m\kappa$,
$m=0$ is an additional degenerate zero-transport stratum.

The retained first-order parameter-lift obstruction at $m=3$ is checked by
both recursive prolongation and an independent principal-symbol calculation.
Its coefficient witness is $3g\kappa$ in each scalar model. This is an exact
restricted obstruction statement about the specified tangency problem with
arbitrary smooth point coefficients. It is not a maximal classification of
all approximate, conditional or nonlocal symmetries \cite{tarayrah2023}.

\section{The surviving common scaling and adapted chart}
Common scaling
\begin{equation}
X_c=t\partial_t+s\partial_s+\phi\partial_\phi
\end{equation}
has first-derivative weight zero, second-derivative weight $-1$, and
third-derivative weight $-2$. Therefore, for arbitrary $m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:commonidentity}
\pr^{(3)}X_c\E_m^{\mathrm p}=-2\E_m^{\mathrm p},\qquad
\pr^{(3)}X_c\E_m^{\mathrm c}=-2\E_m^{\mathrm c}.
\end{equation}
On $t>0$, use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chart}
y=\log t,\qquad z=s/t,\qquad v=\phi/t,
\qquad (t,s,\phi)=(e^y,ze^y,ve^y).
\end{equation}
The base and full old-to-new Jacobians are $t^{-2}$ and $t^{-3}$.
They are nonzero on the chart. Direct actions give $X_cy=1$ and
$X_cz=X_cv=0$. The characteristic is
$Q=\phi-t\phi_t-s\phi_s$. Its first and second total derivatives are included
in the third-order invariant-jet certificate.

The invariant graph is $\phi=tF(z)$. Its useful derivatives are
\begin{align}
\phi_t&=V=F-zP,&\phi_s&=P,\\
\phi_{tt}&=t^{-1}z^2C,&\phi_{ts}&=-t^{-1}zC,&
\phi_{ss}&=t^{-1}C.
\end{align}
Here $P=F'$, $C=F''$, $H=F'''$. With
\begin{equation}
A_\star=C\left(z^2P-\frac g{P^2}\right),\qquad
J_\star=\frac{zV^{m-1}C}{P},
\end{equation}
the exact reductions are
\begin{align}
\E_m^{\mathrm p}[tF]&=t^{-2}\R_m^{\mathrm p}[F],&
\R_m^{\mathrm p}&=-A_\star-zA_\star'+g\beta J_\star',\label{eq:Rp}\\
\E_m^{\mathrm c}[tF]&=t^{-2}\R_m^{\mathrm c}[F],&
\R_m^{\mathrm c}&=\R_m^{\mathrm p}-\left(\frac{VA_\star}{P}\right)'.\label{eq:Rc}
\end{align}
This lowers the independent-variable dimension from two to one. It still
produces a third-order ODE.

\section{Exact first integrals for general transport exponent}
\begin{proposition}[Separate first-integral coefficients]\label{prop:integral}
On $P>0$, $V>0$, define
\begin{align}
L_m^{\mathrm p}&=-z^3P+\frac{gz}{P^2}
 +\frac{g\beta zV^{m-1}}P,\label{eq:Lp}\\
L_m^{\mathrm c}&=F\left(\frac g{P^3}-z^2\right)
 +\frac{g\beta zV^{m-1}}P.\label{eq:Lc}
\end{align}
For the corresponding profile equations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:firstintegral}
\R_m^{\mathrm p}=\left(L_m^{\mathrm p}F''\right)',\qquad
\R_m^{\mathrm c}=\left(L_m^{\mathrm c}F''\right)'.
\end{equation}
With the benchmark convention, replace $\beta$ by $m\kappa$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Rp} is the derivative of
$-zA_\star+g\beta J_\star$. Factoring out $C$ gives
Equation~\eqref{eq:Lp}. Subtracting the derivative of $VA_\star/P$ gives the
corrected integral. Since $z+V/P=F/P$, its nontransport factor is
$-FA_\star/P=F(g/P^3-z^2)C$, proving Equation~\eqref{eq:Lc}.
\end{proof}
This argument is independent of the Lie quotient: it lowers ODE order by
one after the quotient has been obtained. The apparently third-derivative
coefficient is precisely $L_m$. On a connected region where $L_m\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:secondorder}
F'=P,\qquad P'=I_0/L_m(z,F,P),\qquad
I_0=L_m(z_0,F_0,P_0)C_0.
\end{equation}
Only two unknown state variables remain, but the third Cauchy datum has not
been discarded: it fixes the integration constant. If $L_m=0$, this normal
form fails. Singular profiles require a separate analysis rather than a
small-denominator numerical workaround.

\section{Bed reconstruction and verified coupled lifts}
Momentum yields a bed profile
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bedprofile}
b(t,s)=B_\star(z)+\frac{I_0}{g}\log t+b_0,
\qquad B_\star'(z)=-A_\star(z)/g.
\end{equation}
The appropriate printed or corrected first integral ensures its corresponding
transport equation. In particular, the corrected first integral supplies a
constructed solution of Equations~\eqref{eq:momentum}--\eqref{eq:bed}, on its
regular chart. A general elimination-based scalar solution would still need
local compatibility and potential reconstruction; the explicit profile

For the corrected coupled equations, prolongation verifies the following
identities with the displayed bed coefficients:
\begin{align}
\widehat X_c&=t\partial_t+s\partial_s+\phi\partial_\phi,
&\pr\widehat X_cM&=-M,&\pr\widehat X_cB&=-B,\\
\widehat X_5&=t\partial_t-2s\partial_s-2b\partial_b,
&\pr\widehat X_5M&=0,&

The bed shift $\partial_b$ is also admitted. The bare $X_5$, with zero bed

\section{Hard Cauchy data and smooth remainder models}
For Cauchy derivatives $a_j=F^{(j)}(z_0)$ through order $K$, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hardprofile}
\widehat F_\theta(z)=\sum_{j=0}^K\frac{a_j}{j!}(z-z_0)^j
 +(z-z_0)^{K+1}N_\theta(z;\lambda,a).
\end{equation}
Leibniz's rule shows that the remainder and its first $K$ derivatives vanish
at $z_0$, so the traces are exact for every parameter vector. The present
third-order experiments use $K=2$. This construction is related to classical
trial solutions that build data into the approximation \cite{lagaris}.
It enforces Cauchy traces at a single endpoint. Independent Dirichlet data at
a second endpoint require another constraint construction; they are not
implied by the factor in Equation~\eqref{eq:hardprofile}.

The PINN remainder is a two-hidden-layer, width-twelve hyperbolic-tangent
network. The PIKAN remainder uses trainable Chebyshev-polynomial edge
functions and their compositions. Both use float64 arithmetic. Polynomial
edges are smooth through third order and avoid the knot differentiability
issues of low-degree spline models. The label PIKAN here identifies this
physics-informed smooth polynomial-edge architecture; no conclusion about
all KAN implementations follows.

A parameter-conditioned network takes normalized $z$, $m$ and
$\eta_{\mathrm{ic}}$ as inputs. Its Taylor prior also depends on the curvature
parameter, ensuring the correct Cauchy trace for each family member. It learns
only the regular remainder, not a replacement for the symbolic chart or a
proof that arbitrary data admit an invariant solution.

\section{The transformed residual metric}
Suppose the residual in original coordinates satisfies
$\Delta[\widehat u](x)=M(y,z,w)\R[\widehat F](z)$ and the inverse base map has
Jacobian $J$. Transforming the physical residual measure gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric}
W_H(z,w)=\int_{\Omega_y(z)}M^{\mathsf T}M\,|J|\,\dd y.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal L(\theta)=\int_{\Omega_z}\R[\widehat F_\theta]^{\mathsf T}
 W_H(z,w)\R[\widehat F_\theta]\,\dd z
 +w_B\norm{\overline{\mathcal B}[\widehat F_\theta]}^2.
\end{equation}
When the residual has a nontrivial scaling or the chart depends on profile
jets, an unweighted quotient residual can change the optimization problem.
The generic metric API accepts vector residuals and a quadrature over the
orbit variable; the current scalar experiments have constant positive weights.

Using $(t,z)$ for quadrature, the common chart has $\E=t^{-2}\R$ and inverse
Jacobian $t$. Therefore its weight on $t\in[1,2]$ is
\begin{equation}
W_c=\int_1^2t^{-3}\dd t=3/8.
\end{equation}
The cubic chart has $\E=t^{-1}\R$ and Jacobian $t^{-2}$, giving
\begin{equation}
W_5=\int_1^2t^{-4}\dd t=7/24.
\end{equation}
Using $y=\log t$ instead would require the corresponding $\dd y$ Jacobian;
one must not mix the two measures. The benchmark independently integrates the
original-coordinate factor and compares it to the quotient weight.

\section{Exact differential linearization and parameter directions}
For a scalar third-order residual $\R(z,F,P,C,H)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linearization}
D_F\R[\widehat F]h=\R_Fh+\R_Ph'+\R_Ch''+\R_Hh'''.
\end{equation}
The partial coefficients are symbolically derived and compiled; derivatives
of $h$ are obtained from the neural computational graph. For a parameter
direction $\dot\theta$, let $h=D_\theta\widehat F\,\dot\theta$. If the metric
depends on the profile jets, the full loss derivative is
\begin{align}\label{eq:directionalloss}
D_\theta\mathcal L\,\dot\theta
={}&\int\left[2\R^{\mathsf T}W_H D_F\R[h]
 +\R^{\mathsf T}(D_FW_H[h])\R\right]\dd z\\
&+2w_B\left\langle\overline{\mathcal B},
 D_F\overline{\mathcal B}[h]\right\rangle.\notag
\end{align}
For constant $W_H$, the metric-direction term vanishes. The code supplies
both the profile-direction calculation and a parameter-direction
Jacobian--vector product. Unit tests compare the complete directional formula
with automatic differentiation of the scalar loss, including a
profile-dependent test metric. This certifies agreement of the implemented
linearizations at the tested inputs, not optimizer convergence.

\section{A conditional continuous error estimate}\label{sec:conditional-error}
Write the regular third-order ODE as $U'=G(z,U)$, where $U=(F,P,C)$.
Let a $C^3$ trial $\widehat U$ and an exact solution $U$ remain inside a common
convex regular tube. Assume $G$ is Lipschitz in $U$ with constant $\ell$ on
that tube and $|L_m|\geq\delta>0$. For the cubic chart use its own leading
factor in place of $L_m$. The defect is
\begin{equation}
\widehat U'-G(z,\widehat U)=(0,0,\R[\widehat F]/L_m).
\end{equation}
Subtracting the two systems and applying Gronwall gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gronwall}
\norm{\widehat U(z)-U(z)}\leq e^{\ell h}\norm{\widehat U(z_0)-U(z_0)}
 +\frac1\delta\int_{z_0}^{z}e^{\ell(z-r)}|\R[\widehat F](r)|\dd r,
\quad h=z-z_0.
\end{equation}
For hard exact traces, the first term vanishes. Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the
integral by
$\sqrt{(e^{2\ell h}-1)/(2\ell)}\norm{\R}_{L^2}/\delta$, with limit
$\sqrt h\norm{\R}_{L^2}/\delta$ at $\ell=0$.
This uses standard ODE stability reasoning \cite{teschl2012}. It becomes a
numerical certificate only after continuous bounds for the tube, $\ell$,
$\delta$ and the residual integral are supplied. Sampled residuals, a small
training loss, and held-out tests do not supply those hypotheses.
More general PINN error estimates similarly require stability and
approximation assumptions \cite{mishra2023}.

and leading-factor checks guard every evaluated RHS. These checks prevent
silent division through singularities; they do not prove a uniform tube.

A dense state output contains independently interpolated state components.
Taking its interpolated $C$ and reconstructing $H$ from the ODE can make the
residual artificially tiny without differentiating the returned scalar
profile. Accordingly, the benchmark differentiates the actual DOP853 or
Radau scalar dense polynomial through order three. It reports this residual
separately from state convergence. In particular, a low-degree Radau dense
polynomial may have a much weaker third-derivative residual than its state
accuracy suggests.
These are piecewise polynomial residual diagnostics. Possible derivative
jumps between interpolation segments are not included as distributional
defects, and the scalar dense interpolant is not assumed globally $C^3$.
The conditional smooth-trial estimate in Section~\ref{sec:conditional-error} does not apply to that
interpolant without additional regularity checks.

\section{Square nonlinear Chebyshev control}
For total degree $N\in\{8,12,16,24\}$, represent
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral}
F_N=P_2(z)+(z-1)^3\sum_{j=0}^{N-3}c_jT_j(x(z)),
\qquad x(z)=4(z-1)-1.
\end{equation}
There are $N-2$ unknown coefficients. The residual is collocated at $N-2$
Chebyshev--Gauss nodes in the interval. This is a square nonlinear system,
not least-squares fitting of an IVP output. The Jacobian uses
Equation~\eqref{eq:linearization} and analytic derivatives of the basis.
The solver records the optimizer flag and final collocation residual; a
small final residual can be accepted even if an optimizer termination flag
reflects stalled iteration. The evaluation-grid accuracy gates remain
separate and stricter evidence for the selected control.

The constant Taylor prior is the initial spectral guess. No reference values
are used to fit the coefficients. Degree variation supplies a convergence
control; unsuccessful degree attempts remain in the experiment JSON.

\end{equation}
Both common charts use

\end{equation}
Classical cases cover $\eta_{\mathrm{ic}}\in\{-0.1,0,0.1\}$.
For common scaling the exponent set is
\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}
This gives 39 cases and 312
method/tolerance comparisons. This finite set is not an exhaustive parameter

Fixed neural runs use $m=3$, all three charts, all three curvature

Each model has at most 500 Adam updates, 250 L-BFGS closure evaluations and

runs. Loss quadrature uses 32 Gauss nodes per training pair. These budgets
are reproducible experimental controls, not evidence of an impossibility
result when a candidate fails.

\section{Accuracy, reconstruction and selection gates}
For each returned scalar profile the pipeline records maximum profile error,
derivative errors, unnormalized reduced and original scaled residuals,
original residual $L^2$, initial-trace error, first-integral drift where
applicable, minimum sampled slope and velocity, and minimum absolute leading
factor. It independently compares the original-coordinate and reduced
residual formulas on the trial, including non-solutions.

Accuracy admission requires maximum profile error at most $10^{-6}$ and
original residual $L^2$ at most $10^{-4}$, together with valid sampled guards.
A low optimization loss is not itself this admission test. The original
reference state is used as the numerical oracle, with its cross-solver
uncertainty recorded separately. Physical query work includes derivatives,
velocity, depth, bed quadrature and residual reconstruction. The numerical
bed integral in the query is a reconstruction cost; it is not a new exact
bed-error bound.

\section{Complexity records with unknown quantities}
The selector distinguishes independent-variable count, profile-field count,
actual differential order and residual count. A third-order residual
collocation has order three even when the equation has a first integral; the
integrated reference has order two plus a data-selected constant. Chart
margins, leading-factor margins, stability constants and approximation bounds
have different meanings. Unknown constants remain null or unresolved in a
decision record, rather than being filled with zero.

An admissible candidate must first pass model, chart, data and accuracy
gates. Cost ranking then compares the candidates available for a specified
workload. Parameter-family approximation is motivated by repeated queries
\cite{cohen2015}, but a conditioned neural model does not automatically gain
an amortized advantage merely because it accepts parameters as inputs.

\section{Break-even arithmetic and workload choice}
Let $T_N,T_C$ be neural and classical setup costs and $q_N,q_C$ be full-query
costs. Neural work is no more expensive at query count $n$ when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
T_N+nq_N\leq T_C+nq_C.
\end{equation}
If $q_C>q_N$, the first admissible integer crossing is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:break}
N_{\mathrm{break}}=\max\left(0,
\left\lceil\frac{T_N-T_C}{q_C-q_N}\right\rceil\right).
\end{equation}
The code checks the resulting inequality to protect against floating-point
rounding at an integer boundary. If the neural query is equal or slower,
there is no asymptotic speedup. A lower neural setup with a slower query can
provide only a finite early-query advantage; this is recorded separately.

For repeated queries to one fixed profile, the appropriate control is a
cached spectral expansion. Charging it a fresh integration on every query
would bias the comparison. For new parameters, a fresh spectral nonlinear
solve is included. Neural setup includes training and reference validation;
query time includes third derivatives, guards and physical reconstruction.
A rejected candidate incurs a classical fallback. Failed candidates remain
accuracy-inadmissible regardless of their evaluation speed.

Timing uses a warm-up and repeated measurements, recording median, minimum
and maximum. The same sampled grids and reconstruction work are used when
comparing admitted candidates. A crossing extrapolates measured query costs;
it is conditional on future queries having comparable cost and maintaining
accuracy. Four held-out pairs cannot prove that every future parameter query
will satisfy those assumptions. Consequently, the campaign can identify
observed conditional amortization but not a production guarantee.

\section{Implications for neural profile learning}
Neural learning is computationally justified only if a candidate passes the
matched accuracy gates, its workload has a positive per-query saving, and
the expected query count exceeds a defensible measured crossing. This is a
stricter conclusion than stating that symmetry reduces input dimension or
that an optimizer reaches a small training residual. Symmetry-informed
learning studies motivate comparisons with unconstrained neural solvers
\cite{sympinn}; the present campaign instead tests whether learning is needed
at all after a verified reduction exposes an inexpensive classical profile
problem.

For a small smooth interval with only one profile, the cached spectral
control has a strong computational case. For a wider parameter family,
conditioning can amortize setup, but it adds validation burden and may
reduce accuracy. The complete recorded results decide these trade-offs for
the declared workload, with failed runs preserved. Extending budgets or
changing architectures is a new experiment and must not overwrite this
campaign's negative evidence.

\chapter{Conclusions and research directions}\label{ch:conclusion}
\section{Established results}
The primary established result is the independent audit of the printed
scalar SVE source, its corrected local reductions and constructed solutions.
The first-principles reducer keeps prolongation, exceptional parameter
strata, invariant-graph rank, chart denominators and data compatibility
separate. Common scaling survives in both declared scalar models for the
stated positive branch; their verified general-exponent first integrals are
Equations~\eqref{eq:Lp} and \eqref{eq:Lc}. These mathematical integrals are
not learned outputs. Selected corrected bed-system lifts are checked, but
no maximal coupled-system classification follows.

The strongest change prompted by the boundary reference is the explicit
separation of equation symmetry, boundary tangency and data compatibility.
Moving rays are useful for common scaling; fixed positive-time traces and
nonzero fixed walls are counterexamples. Local solvability is treated with
Olver's nondegeneracy hypotheses, rather than elevated into a claim about
arbitrary continuous-space boundary data.

\section{Proposed order-reduction stage and unresolved questions}
The next learning question is whether a model can propose a nontrivial
$I(z,F,P,C)$ or an admitted reduced-ODE generator more usefully than direct
symbolic search. Equation~\eqref{eq:dissertation-learned-I}, independence,
regular chart and data checks decide acceptance. Total training, symbolic
verification and repeated-query costs must be compared with direct algebra
and one classical ODE solve. The historical profile-fitting runs do not
answer this question; their negative outcomes remain in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

A maximal symmetry classification of the corrected coupled system remains
open in this implementation. So do weak/shock solutions, arbitrary physical
IBVP well-posedness, global chart coverage and parameter-uniform error bounds
for the learned profiles. The extended perturbed benchmark has sampled
regularity checks; it does not inherit the exact unperturbed tube certificate
on a shorter interval.

The current work establishes an auditable engineering and mathematical
baseline. Its priority and novelty have not been compared with current peer
submissions or an online literature survey. The consulted fundamentals and
selected manuscript bibliography support derivations and scope, not a claim
that each technique or formula is newly discovered.

\section{Research agenda}
First compare alternative Lagrange and Eulerian descriptions with explicit
model-equivalence and chart checks; a coordinate option is not a variational
action. Second map the exact exceptional parameter strata and projected
orbit-rank strata of candidate symmetries; no probability distribution is
claimed without a specified sampling law. Third apply the task-dependent
filtration of validity, graph reduction, data preservation and solver value.
A valid symmetry can fail a particular filter without becoming invalid.

A separate analytical goal is a continuous regular-tube and residual-error
certificate for a bounded family. Interval enclosures that cross a singular
factor must return unresolved. A coupled bed-system classification requires
its own unrestricted determining and boundary analysis. Hybrid architecture
design and conservation analogies are deferred beyond these gates. A
variational or Noether claim would require a checked action for the intended
equations, not just the existence of a Lie point symmetry.
