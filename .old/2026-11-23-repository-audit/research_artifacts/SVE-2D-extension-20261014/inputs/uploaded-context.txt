# 1. What is the 2D Saint–Venant–Exner (SVE) system?

The two-dimensional Saint–Venant–Exner system (2D SVE) is a coupled system of nonlinear partial differential equations describing the interaction between:

1. Water flow (hydrodynamics).
2. Sediment transport.
3. Erosion, deposition, and evolution of the riverbed (morphodynamics).

It combines the Saint–Venant shallow-water equations with the Exner sediment conservation equation.

[A Comparative Study of Comprehensive Modeling Systems for Sediment Transport in a Curved Open Channel](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/W58ojBT09_38B6Sguf45dMbIYkSRXVJFVPm1L7Ykjrs7T96TmHxRo3Q6uIx71UkBLwqnyxsxAeuIBPxM5NFFfT2i99MbGFC3N24WdOuSO22lUaEoNH3q2cYU-rUGzLhxg5gTYUKDxQlFAnO4HqKluXXYnxmTeF5ttn6n0m8mhnM?purpose=inline)

The water flows over an erodible bed. The water depth, horizontal velocities, and bed elevation evolve together.

The term two-dimensional means the equations resolve two horizontal spatial coordinates \\(x,y\\), together with time \\(t\\). The vertical water column is depth-averaged.

Consequently, this is a (2+1)-dimensional PDE system, not a fully three-dimensional fluid model.

## 1.1 Governing equations

Let

\\[ \mathbf u=(u,v),\qquad h=h(x,y,t),\qquad z_b=z_b(x,y,t). \\]

A standard four-equation 2D SVE model is

\\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} h_t+(hu)\_x+(hv)\_y&=0,\\\\[2pt] (hu)\_t+ \left(hu^2+\frac12gh^2\right)\_x+ (huv)\_y &=-gh(z_b)\_x-ghS\_{fx},\\\\[2pt] (hv)\_t+ (huv)\_x+ \left(hv^2+\frac12gh^2\right)\_y &=-gh(z_b)\_y-ghS\_{fy},\\\\[2pt] (1-p)(z_b)\_t+ (q\_{bx})\_x+ (q\_{by})\_y&=0. \end{aligned} } \\]

This is the conventional depth-averaged, bedload-only formulation, with bed-slope and friction forces.&#x20;

The four PDEs represent one water-mass conservation law, two momentum balances, and one sediment-mass conservation law.

## 1.2 The Exner equation: why the bed evolves

The final equation is especially important:

\\[ \boxed{ (1-p)\frac{\partial z_b}{\partial t} =-\nabla\cdot\mathbf q_b. } \\]

It says the riverbed changes according to the divergence of the sediment flux.

- When \\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q_b>0\\), more sediment leaves a region than enters it, so the bed erodes.
- When \\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q_b<0\\), sediment accumulates, so the bed rises.

For example, suppose

\\[ q\_{bx}=q_0+ax,\qquad q\_{by}=by, \\]

with \\(a,b\\) constant. Then

\\[ \frac{\partial z_b}{\partial t} =-\frac{a+b}{1-p}. \\]

If \\(a+b>0\\), the bed elevation decreases linearly with time.

This mathematical conservation principle is what allows SVE models to predict morphological change rather than merely simulate water movement.

## 1.3 The sediment-transport closure

The conservation equations alone are insufficient. We need a constitutive relationship for the sediment flux.

One commonly used idealization is a Grass-type power law:

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbf q_b =\kappa |\mathbf u|^{m-1}\mathbf u } \\]

where \\(\kappa\\) is a transport coefficient and \\(m\\) is a transport exponent.

In components,

\\[ \begin{aligned} q\_{bx}&=\kappa(u^2+v^2)^{(m-1)/2}u,\\\ q\_{by}&=\kappa(u^2+v^2)^{(m-1)/2}v. \end{aligned} \\]

The frequently used cubic Grass closure corresponds to \\(m=3\\). More physical closures, such as Meyer–Peter–Müller, account for the critical shear stress required to mobilize sediment.&#x20;

# 3. What changes mathematically from 1D to 2D?

| Property              | 1D SVE                                        | 2D SVE                                        |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Independent variables | \\(t,x\\)                                     | \\(t,x,y\\)                                   |
| Main unknowns         | \\(h,u,z_b\\)                                 | \\(h,u,v,z_b\\)                               |
| PDE count             | 3                                             | 4                                             |
| Velocity              | Scalar                                        | Vector                                        |
| Sediment flux         | Scalar                                        | Vector                                        |
| Bed evolution         | Longitudinal                                  | Longitudinal and transverse                   |
| Symmetry analysis     | Vector fields on 5-dimensional variable space | Vector fields on 7-dimensional variable space |

For instance, imposing

\\[ \partial_y h=\partial_y u=\partial_y z_b=0, \qquad v=0 \\]

and compatible \\(y\\)-independent conditions reduces the 2D model to its familiar 1D counterpart.

Conversely, the full 2D model permits transverse instabilities, sediment bars, and channel bifurcations that a purely 1D model cannot represent.

# 4. Implications for your Lie-symmetry and SVE research

Your current research studies symmetry reduction near an exceptional sediment-transport exponent. Extending this program to 2D is mathematically meaningful, but introduces significant new constraints.

For the 2D system, the general Lie point-symmetry generator is

\\[ \boxed{ X=\tau\partial_t+\xi\partial_x+\zeta\partial_y +\eta^h\partial_h+\eta^u\partial_u +\eta^v\partial_v+\eta^b\partial\_{z_b}. } \\]

All seven coefficients may depend on

\\[ (t,x,y,h,u,v,z_b). \\]

A particularly interesting candidate is the infinitesimal planar rotation

\\[ R=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y -v\partial_u+u\partial_v. \\]

For an isotropic sediment closure and rotationally invariant governing equations, \\(R\\) generates a genuine rotational symmetry. Boundaries and anisotropic forcing can destroy it.

This gives a possible analytical pathway:

2D SVE system

Four coupled nonlinear PDEs

Verify a Lie symmetry

Prolongation and determining equations

Construct invariant coordinates

Reduce 2 spatial variables to 1 where possible

Reduced PDE system

Further symmetry reduction may produce ODEs

Analytical or semi-analytical approximation

Series, classical solvers, or neural approximation

One important distinction: a single symmetry in a \\((2+1)\\)-dimensional system ordinarily reduces the number of independent variables from three to two. It does not automatically reduce the system to ODEs. A second compatible reduction, such as a scaling or traveling-wave symmetry, may be needed.

Furthermore, the exceptional \\(m=3\\) symmetry of a particular 1D Lagrangian formulation cannot automatically be assumed to survive in the 2D Eulerian equations. The determining equations must be recomputed.

A relevant mathematical complication is that some 2D morphodynamic models become ill-posed under certain sediment closures and transverse-slope assumptions. This is analyzed in the Journal of Fluid Mechanics paper [Ill-posedness in modelling two-dimensional morphodynamic problems: effects of bed slope and secondary flow](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-fluid-mechanics/article/ill-posedness-in-modelling-twodimensional-morphodynamic-problems-effects-of-bed-slope-and-secondary-flow/08209F5A84D3444B17BB05C8717D9A63).&#x20;

## 5. Recommended research direction

For your Lie–ML project, I would treat 2D SVE as a second-stage extension, rather than replacing the current 1D formulation immediately.

The most informative first test would be a simplified frictionless, flat-reference-bed 2D SVE model with an isotropic Grass closure. Determine its point-symmetry algebra, verify its translational and rotational generators, and construct a nontrivial invariant reduction. Then compare a classical numerical solution, a Lie-reduced solution, and a semi-analytical neural approximation under the same initial and boundary conditions.

This isolates the analytical contribution before introducing friction, lateral slope corrections, and more realistic sediment physics.

The central research opportunity: 2D SVE introduces additional geometric structure—particularly rotational invariance, transverse modes, and multidimensional conservation laws—that your Lie-symmetry framework could exploit, provided those structures are verified rather than assumed.
